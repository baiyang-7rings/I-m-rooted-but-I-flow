# Epistolary Paper

## Design Philosophy

**Epistolary Paper** is a visual philosophy rooted in the materiality of correspondence—the creased envelope, the hand-addressed card, the polaroid tucked between pages. It finds beauty in the quiet, unassuming object: a cream-colored rectangle of paper with a thin black border, placed at a slight angle, pinned with a single imperfect circle. The space around each card is as important as the card itself; negative air breathes between objects like pauses in a letter. Every composition is a meticulously curated desk surface, where artifacts of identity sit in deliberate asymmetry—a photograph here, a name card there, a small stamp as punctuation.

Space and form follow the logic of the physical desk: rectangular cards of varying sizes, each slightly rotated (never more than five degrees), overlapping with the casual carelessness of someone who has just set them down. The background is the paper itself—#F2F2F1, a warm off-white that holds ink without glare. Form is honest and unadorned: no shadows that do not exist in reality, no gradients that a real piece of paper wouldn't cast. The border is a single black line, drawn with the slight waver of a human hand, never perfectly straight yet never careless. Each card is an artifact of meticulous craft, placed with the precision of a master calligrapher arranging tools on a workbench—the product of deep expertise and painstaking attention.

Color and material are deliberately restricted. The palette is nearly monochrome: cream paper, black ink, the desaturated tones of a photograph embedded within. When a photographic image appears, it sits within its paper frame like a polaroid—bordered by the cream stock, not bleeding to edges. Material honesty dictates that a photograph looks like a photograph (printed, slightly faded at edges), text looks like text (typewritten, not typeset), and paper looks like paper (matte, toothy, with the subtle imperfection of fiber). Nothing is glossy, nothing is digital in appearance. Every pixel is placed with master-level execution, as though someone at the absolute top of their field spent countless hours calibrating the exact weight of each line and the precise tone of the paper surface.

Scale and rhythm follow the tempo of a personal letter. Cards vary in size from small stamps (roughly 4:1 aspect ratio) to notecards (roughly 2:1) to portrait photographs (roughly 3:4). Their arrangement suggests a narrative: a name card introduces the person, a photograph shows them, a small tag bears a single essential detail. Rhythm is created through rotation—one card tilts left at 3 degrees, another tilts right at 2 degrees—creating visual counterpoint without chaos. Scale relationships are harmonious: the largest card is never more than 2.5 times the size of the smallest, maintaining visual kinship among the family of objects. The composition breathes with the ease of something labored over with care, not assembled quickly.

Composition and balance reject perfect symmetry in favor of the naturally asymmetrical. Cards cluster on one side with the confidence of a well-composed letter—the reader's eye travels from the largest element (envelope/name card) to the secondary (photograph) to the smallest (stamp/tag). A pin circle—simple, gray, hand-drawn with slight irregularity—anchors the top of each card to the imaginary surface. Balance is achieved through visual weight, not mathematical centering: a dense cluster of three cards on the left is balanced by one wider card on the right, each carrying equal conviction. The entire arrangement is the product of countless hours of refinement, painstaking attention to every spatial relationship, and master-level execution of every detail.

Visual hierarchy communicates through size, position, and rotation—not through bold color or dramatic contrast. The eye moves as it would across a desk: first to the tilted photograph with its recognizable human subject, then to the card bearing a name in typewriter characters, finally to the small tag with contact details. Text is sparse, typed, essential: a name, a role, a WeChat ID, no biographies, no explanations. Typography uses a typewriter face (Special Elite) for English text—monospaced, slightly uneven, with the character of something struck onto paper by metal keys—and for Chinese text, a simple sans-serif that respects the typewriter aesthetic without pretending to be one. Every kerning decision, every point size, is the result of meticulous calibration by an expert hand.
